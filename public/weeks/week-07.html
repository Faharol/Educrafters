05 · Method

How to read a figure: six steps, in this order

  1. Read the title, units and period

    What is counted, in what unit, and when? Counts or rates? One year or several?

  2. Check the axes

    Does the value axis start at zero? If not, the differences may look bigger than they are.

  3. Find the biggest (and smallest)

    Which category is largest now?

  4. Find the biggest change

    As a number and as a percentage. They are not always the same category.

  5. Look for a break in the pattern

    Did the order change? Did one category pass another?

  6. Look for what goes the other way

    Which categories moved against the overall trend?

Steps 4 and 5 are where the good sentences come from. A student who reports only totals writes a dull paragraph. A student who spots the change in order writes something a manager would want to read. Write the six steps on a card and keep it beside you for the rest of the session.

09 · Writing

From figure to finding: three sentence patterns

  1. The overall picture, then the detail

    “Total recorded injuries rose by 7.0% between 2022 and 2024, from 129 to 138. This overall figure, however, hides large differences between categories.”

  2. The most important change, with the numbers

    “The sharpest increase was in falls from height, which rose by 12 incidents (+35.3%) and overtook manual handling as the leading cause of injury.”

  3. The part that goes the other way

    “Three of the five categories fell over the same period, with struck-by-object incidents down 11.1% and electrical injuries down 12.5%.”

  4. Put together, that is your findings section

    Three sentences, about 70 words: the overall movement, the most important change, and the part that goes the other way. This template works for almost any figure.

Pattern 1 uses “however, hides”: the linking move that tells the reader you understood the data instead of just reading it off.

13 · Check your answer

A model recommendation

Show the model recommendation
“It is recommended that compulsory fall-arrest training be introduced first. At RM 60,000 and three months it is the cheapest and fastest of the three options, and it addresses falls from height, which the data show to be the main cause of the increase. Edge protection should follow in the next financial year, as it gives the largest reduction but costs considerably more. Wearable sensors should be delayed: they cost more than training, take the longest, give the smallest reduction, and apply to high-rise sites only.”

Value and speed

Training is first for value and speed, not because it works best. Edge protection reduces falls more, but costs 3.5 times as much and takes nearly twice as long.

A sequence

A sequence beats picking one winner.

Three grounds

The delay is justified on three grounds at once. That is what a strong justification looks like.

Arguing for edge protection first, because falls are the most serious kind of injury and worth the money, is also right, as long as you say so. The justification earns the marks, not the choice.

14 · Common faults

What weakens writing about visual data

01

Describing every number

You are writing a report, not a list. Choose only the important information.

02

Only a percentage, or only an amount

“Landscaping rose 22.7%” tells the reader nothing until they know it rose by 5 units. Use both when they help.

03

A number the chart does not show

If there are no data labels, write “approximately”.

04

“The figure below”

Refer by number: “Figure 1 shows…”. Layout changes when a document is edited, but numbers do not.

Spoken language

“I think they should…” belongs in discussion, not in a formal report. See Week 6.

There is no case for a 3-D pie chart in a technical document. The same applies to 3-D bars, and to any chart with a shadow or a tilt. “It looks more professional”? It makes the reader work harder and trust you less.

30 · Misleading

Three more ways a chart misleads

01

A convenient starting point

Starting the series at an unusually low point makes any later rise look bigger than it is. Show the full relevant series.

02

Two different scales

Two lines on two different scales can be made to look connected just by choosing the scales. Use them only when necessary, and explain why.

03

Area for one number

If both width and height double, the area becomes four times larger for twice the value. Readers judge area, not width.

The test: would the people represented by the data recognise the chart as a fair picture of their results?

Two-scale charts appear constantly in news graphics. Now you know they are a known trap.

34 · Colour

Colour: what it is for

Organising

Grouping related items, showing levels.

Signalling

Drawing attention to the one thing that matters.

Carrying meaning

As in a chart series or a heat map.

Guidelines

  • Choose a small set of colours and use them the same way in every visual in the document.
  • Use colour sparingly. If everything is highlighted, nothing is.
  • Keep dark text on light backgrounds. Light text on dark is harder to read, and worse when printed.
  • Bars, lines and slices should differ in colour from the axes and gridlines.
  • Always give a key, or better, label the series directly on the chart. Direct labelling removes the back-and-forth eye movement a legend forces.

Contrast: what passes and what does not

WCAG 2.1 asks for at least 4.5:1 for normal text, and 3:1 for large text and for non-text parts such as chart lines and bars.

Try your own colours

Light grey and orange both look readable on a bright screen, and both fail on a projector or in print. This is why accent colours belong in headings and graphics, not body text. Source: W3C (2018), Web Content Accessibility Guidelines 2.1.

45 · Credit

Crediting a visual you did not make

You copied someone's chart or photograph

Reproduce it, credit it underneath, and give the full reference. Check first whether the licence allows you to reproduce it at all.

You redrew their chart from their data

Credit the data: Data from [3] or Note. Data from ILO (2024). You made the figure; they made the data.

You changed their diagram

Adapted from [5] or Note. Adapted from …. Say what you changed if it matters.

You made it yourself from your own data

No source credit needed, but still number it, caption it, and say where the data came from.

It is a stock photograph or an icon set

Check the licence. Many require attribution; some do not allow use in printed materials.

It is a screenshot of software

Credit the software and the version. Some licences restrict screenshots in publications.

46 · Generative AI

AI-generated images in technical documents

Not acceptable
  • As evidence. An AI image of a process, a part or a site shows nothing that exists, so it cannot support a claim.
  • As a chart or data figure. Never let a generative tool draw your data. The numbers will not be yours.
  • Where a publisher forbids them. Many journals now do.
  • Where the image shows a real person, a real site or a real product.
Maybe: check with your lecturer first

Purely decorative or conceptual illustration, clearly labelled as such, where nothing depends on it being real.

If you use one, cite it and declare it

APA reference: OpenAI. (2025). DALL·E [Image model]. https://openai.com/

APA figure note: Note. Image generated using the prompt “[your exact prompt]”, by OpenAI, DALL·E, 2025 (https://openai.com/).

IEEE: [8] DALL·E. OpenAI. Accessed: Aug. 18, 2026. [Online]. Available: https://openai.com/

Why can't an AI image of “a worker wearing a safety harness” support your safety report?

Put the exact prompt in the note: the same prompt produces a different image every time.

47 · Placement

Placing a visual in your text: introduce, show, explain

  1. Introduce it before it appears

    “Figure 1 compares recorded injuries by cause for 2022 and 2024.”

    The reader should know what they are about to look at.

  2. Show it close to the text that discusses it

    Less than half a page: on the same page. More than half a page: immediately after. Complex or extra material: in an appendix, with the page number given.

  3. Explain the main point afterwards

    “As Figure 1 shows, falls from height became the leading cause.”

    A visual dropped in with no follow-up text is decoration.

One visual, one point. If your figure supports three arguments at once, it probably needs to be three figures. Most students do only the middle step.

51 · Layout

Layout and reading path

Most readers go down the first column, then the second, then the third. Put your sections in that order.

The empty band at the bottom of the sketch is deliberate: it shows what about 70% coverage looks like. White space is part of the design.

Two quick tests before you print

  • Stand two metres back: is the topic clear?
  • Print in black and white: is every visual still readable?
52 · Poster captions

Captions and credits on a poster

  • Every visual needs a number, a caption and a source, the same as in a report.
  • Put the credit directly underneath the visual, not in a footnote at the bottom of the poster.
  • Keep captions to one line where you can. Space on a poster is expensive.
  • The caption should say what the visual shows, not just what it is.
  • If the meaning is not clear within about three seconds, it needs a better caption, or a different visual.
  • A QR code linking to your full reference list is fine, but the credits on the poster itself still have to be there.

53 · Checklist

Check every visual against this list

  • It answers a question the reader actually has.
  • It is the right type for that question.
  • It has a number, a specific caption and a source.
  • Table captions above; figure captions below.
  • It is referred to by number in your text, and explained there.
  • If it is a bar chart, the value axis starts at zero.
  • Units and time period are stated.
  • It passes the black-and-white test, and colour is not the only thing carrying meaning.
  • It is readable at final printed size, at 300 dpi or as vector art.
  • Nothing on it is there only for decoration.
