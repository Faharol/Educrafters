What goes into a graduate résumé?

  1. Name + contact information

  2. Professional summary (optional)

  3. Education

  4. Technical / relevant skills

  5. Experience

    Internship · employment · projects

  6. Achievements / activities / leadership

  7. Certifications / languages / other relevant information

Not every résumé needs exactly the same sections. Relevance determines what stays.

Part 3 · Prove

Include it or leave it out?

Generally avoid unnecessary personal information. The goal is information relevant to your suitability for the position.

✓ Include
✗ Generally avoid

A photograph? Follow local industry norms and employer requirements.

Part 3 · Prove

ACTION + TASK + IMPACT

Don't merely describe responsibilities.

Instead of:

Responsible for testing equipment.

Write:

Conducted performance testing on prototype components and documented results for the engineering team.

Instead of:

Helped organise an event.

Write:

Coordinated registration for a university programme involving 300 participants.

Take one real experience from your checklist (a project, lab, club or job). Write one résumé line using ACTION + TASK + IMPACT. Only use true facts.

Check your answer
  • Does it start with a strong action verb (not “Responsible for …”)?
  • Does it say clearly what the task was?
  • Does it show an impact or result: who benefited, how many, what changed?
  • Is every fact true and something you can explain in an interview?
Part 3 · Prove

Use strong action verbs

Start achievement statements with clear verbs. Avoid repeatedly writing “Responsible for …”.

Technical
Communication
Leadership
See the full verb lists

Technical: Designed · Developed · Tested · Analysed · Programmed · Calculated · Modelled · Evaluated

Communication: Presented · Explained · Documented · Reported · Prepared · Coordinated

Leadership: Led · Managed · Organised · Supervised · Facilitated · Initiated

Part 3 · Prove

Quantify when you can

Numbers provide context. Compare:

Organised a student programme.

with:

Coordinated a student programme involving 120 participants and 15 committee members.

Improved the design.

with:

Redesigned the component, reducing material usage by 12%.

But: NEVER INVENT NUMBERS. Evidence must be accurate and defensible.

You helped at an event but do not know how many people attended. What should you do?

Part 3 · Prove

One résumé for every job? NO.

Your basic information remains the same. But adjust these according to the job:

  • Relevant skills
  • Keywords
  • Experience emphasis
  • Projects
  • Professional summary

Tailoring ≠ lying. Tailoring means making the most relevant truthful information easier to see.

5

Cover letter

Interpret your résumé for the employer.

Part 5 · Cover letter

Do cover letters still matter?

Sometimes yes; sometimes they are not required. Follow the job advertisement. When requested or appropriate, a good cover letter answers three questions:

Why this job?

Why this organisation?

Why you?

Current career guidance still recommends concise, tailored cover letters that connect relevant experience to the specific role.

How do you know whether to send a cover letter?

Part 5 · Cover letter

Open · Connect · Prove · Close

  1. Open

    State the role and your interest.

  2. Connect

    Connect your strongest relevant skills/experience to what the employer needs.

  3. Prove

    Give brief evidence.

  4. Close

    Express interest in discussing your suitability further and thank the reader.

Don't repeat your résumé. Interpret it for the employer.

Recall the four moves in order.

1 → 2 → 3 → 4

Part 5 · Cover letter

Weak vs stronger

Weak

I am hardworking, responsible and a good team player. I believe I am suitable for this position.

Stronger

During my industrial training, I worked with the maintenance team to analyse equipment records and prepare weekly performance reports. This experience strengthened both my analytical and technical communication skills, which align closely with the requirements of the Graduate Engineer position.

Part 6 · AI

The Interview Test

Before you submit anything AI has helped you write, ask yourself:

“If an interviewer asks me about this sentence, can I explain and defend it?”

If the answer is NO, don't submit it.

An AI tool adds “Reduced energy costs by 20%” to your internship bullet. You did not measure this. What do you do?

Part 6 · AI

AI as coach, not ghostwriter

Instead of
“Write my résumé.”
Try
“Here is a Graduate Engineer job advertisement and my actual project experience. Identify the three most relevant skills I should emphasise. Do not invent information.”

Then:

“Review this bullet point for clarity and impact. Preserve all facts and tell me what evidence is missing.”

YOU provide the experience. AI helps you communicate it. Final responsibility for accuracy and authenticity remains yours.

Part 7 · Interview

Before the interview

Research: products/services · projects · industry · values · recent developments.

Re-read: responsibilities · skills · requirements.

Prepare evidence of: technical skills · teamwork · leadership · problem solving · challenges · achievements.

Mock interviews remain highly useful. Practise, and ask for constructive feedback.

  • I can name the organisation's products/services and one recent development.
  • I have re-read the responsibilities and requirements.
  • I have one evidence story each for teamwork, leadership and problem solving.
  • I have done a mock interview and received feedback.
Part 7 · Interview

Don't just SAY it. STAR it.

Behavioural question: “Tell me about a time you solved a difficult problem.” Use:

S

Situation

What was happening?

T

Task

What needed to be done?

A

Action

What did you do?

R

Result

What happened?

STAR remains a widely used framework for structuring evidence-based interview responses.

“What did you do?” belongs to which STAR element?

Challenge Part 7 · Interview

“Tell me about a time you worked successfully in a team.”

S: What was the situation? T: What was your responsibility? A: What did you do? R: What was the outcome?

Check your answer
  • S: a real team situation (project, lab, club, job).
  • T: your responsibility in the team.
  • A: what you did (use “I”, with action verbs).
  • R: a clear outcome, with an accurate number if you have one.

Now say it aloud in 60 seconds. If you have a partner, they listen for the four STAR elements.
