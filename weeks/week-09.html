Why paraphrasing and citation matter

Why paraphrasing matters

Technical writers must synthesise complex proposals, operational logs or equipment manuals into concise prose.

  • The plagiarism danger: copying exact phrases from the visual tables, descriptions of sustainable measures, or text prompts will directly lower your language score.
  • Register preservation: paraphrasing must keep technical rigour. Replace common phrases with specialised engineering and business terms.

Academic and technical citations

  • Citation standards: use the referencing rules from Week 6. When analysing standard operating protocols or policy documents, apply APA or IEEE citation models.
  • Integrating references: frame citations naturally within sentences.

Integrity checklist: every external framework, baseline dataset or industry regulation must be credited in your final report.

From raw proposal to professional report

The power of synthesised prose

  1. Raw proposal text

    “Solar Panel Installation: RM 420,000. 25-30% saving. Takes 18 months. Applies to all departments.”

  2. Weak paraphrase: plagiarised and flat

    “We should do Solar Panel Installation because it costs RM 420,000, saves 25-30% energy, and takes 18 months for all departments.”

    Critique: conversational language; copies the table labels word for word; does not weave the data logically; lacks engineering register.

  3. Strong paraphrase: synthesised and professional

    “Although implementing a facility-wide photovoltaic system requires a substantial capital outlay of RM 420,000 and an 18-month timeline, it offers the highest potential energy reduction of up to 30% across all operations.”

    Strengths: synthesises the facts into one complex sentence; uses professional terms (photovoltaic system, capital outlay); paraphrases the timeline and impact smoothly.

From visual data to a cohesive report

Non-linear to linear translation: four steps, then the three sections of the report.

Process non-linear information systematically

Four steps to translate visual data

01

The big picture

Analyse the overall trajectory. What does the chart show? Look at axes, titles, units and overall changes across quarters.

02

Detect outliers

Find extreme values: which department uses the most, which uses the least, and which shows the sharpest changes.

03

Group the data

Never list data line by line. Group departments that perform similarly. It shows critical analysis and saves words.

04

Bridge to the table

Connect problem to solution. Match the high-consumption areas in the chart with the most targeted sustainable measures.

Section 1 Introduction

Opening the report

Structural blueprint

  • Context & origin: the source context, such as the company name, location or reporting period.
  • Core purpose: why the report is written (e.g. assessing utility consumption trends).
  • Scope of analysis: what is evaluated (e.g. five departments and proposed solutions).
  • Length: very brief, 1 to 2 sentences (about 30–40 words maximum).

Model answer

“This report has been prepared for the Sustainability Committee to analyse electricity consumption patterns across five departments from Q1 to Q2 and evaluate proposed energy-saving measures.”

Why it works: professional context straight away; no personal pronouns (I, we); context, purpose and scope in one fluent sentence.

Conversational openings such as “I am writing this because my boss asked me to” fail the professional register expected at CEFR B2–C1. The passive voice is very useful here.

Section 2 Findings

Report the facts, nothing else

Technical blueprint

  • Overall trajectory: state the big trend. Is consumption increasing, decreasing or stable?
  • Primary outliers: highlight the department that dominates or shows the most critical surge.
  • Comparative evidence: weave in specific values (e.g. m³, MT CO2e) for the main outlier.
  • Objective register: strictly analytical. Verified facts only; keep recommendations for Section 3.

Model answer

“Data reveals an overall upward trend in consumption, driven heavily by the Cooling Towers, which recorded a surge from 800 m³ in Q1 to 950 m³ in Q2. In contrast, administrative usage declined slightly…”

Why it works: synthesises trends instead of copying raw numbers; highlights the critical outlier with precise data; keeps interpretation separate from recommendations.

Section 3 Recommendations

Choose one measure and defend it

Action blueprint

  • Logical selection: recommend exactly one measure that directly addresses the main outlier from Section 2.
  • Technical justification: defend it with the table: estimated cost, savings and timeline.
  • The contrast principle: say why it beats the alternatives (e.g. faster deployment or greater impact).
  • Cautious language: hedge projections (would potentially, indicates).

Model answer

“Given that the Cooling Towers are the primary consumers, retrofitting them with a closed-loop system is highly recommended. Despite the RM 150,000 cost, this measure directly targets our highest-use area and can be operational within four months.”

Why it works: uses the outlier to justify the choice; brings in table numbers (cost and timeline); includes a concession (“Despite…”) for a balanced argument.

This is the analytical climax. A strong recommendation explains why the alternatives were set aside: too expensive, too slow to deploy, or not aimed at the critical problem area.

Hedging

Express probability, not certainty

The power of caution

  • Avoid overclaiming: projections, budgets and savings are estimates, not guaranteed facts.
  • Strategic modals: could, would, may soften assertions.
  • Probability adverbs: potentially, highly likely, presumably create a professional, analytical register.

Hedging is marked under Language Accuracy & Register. Overconfident claims make a report look unscientific or reckless to managers.

Which sentence is professionally hedged?

Rewrite with hedging: “This new pump will cut energy costs and fix the overheating problem.”
