A

Finding and judging sources

Where engineers get their evidence, and how they decide what to believe.

Part A · Why research?

A technical document is only as strong as what stands behind it

Before a recommendation is written, a good engineer has already asked: what do we know, who measured it, and how sure are they?

In the workplace

Site data, supplier specifications, standards, incident reports.

In this course

Journal articles, government reports, and reliable datasets on an SDG-related engineering problem.

The test of a source

Could another engineer follow it back and check it?

When was the last time you had to prove something with numbers, not opinion? Remember: at the poster session, the panel will ask “where did this figure come from?”

Part A · Two kinds of research

Primary and secondary research

Both are research. The difference is who collected the data.

Primary

You collect the data yourself

Experiments, surveys, observation, measurement.

Secondary

Someone else collected it

You use their published findings: journal articles, reports, textbooks, datasets.

Secondary
Part A · Primary research

Four primary research methods engineers use

01

Experiment or test

Change one variable, hold the rest, measure the result. The strongest evidence, and the hardest to set up.

02

Survey or interview

Ask people. Good for opinions, habits and needs. Weak for facts people cannot remember accurately.

03

Observation

Watch what actually happens on site. Reveals the gap between the procedure and the practice.

04

Measurement and logging

Sensors, meters and records. Objective, continuous, and easy to chart.

You suspect workers skip a safety step that is written in the procedure. Which method shows this best?

Part A · Secondary research

Four secondary sources you will use for the poster

01

Peer-reviewed journal articles

Checked by other experts before publication. The gold standard for claims and figures.

02

Reports and standards

Government, industry and standards bodies. Excellent for regulations, baselines and definitions.

03

Textbooks and handbooks

Stable, well-explained fundamentals. Rarely the newest data.

04

Datasets and statistics

DOSM, World Bank, UN. Raw numbers you can chart yourself, with the source stated.

Part A · Judging sources

Five checks before you trust a source

Run all five in under two minutes. If any one fails badly, keep looking.

C

Currency

Is it recent enough for your topic?

A

Authority

Who wrote and published it? Are they qualified to say this?

A

Accuracy

Is it supported by evidence that can be checked?

P

Purpose

Why was it written? To inform, or to sell?

R

Relevance

Does it answer your question?

Purpose is the check students skip. A manufacturer's white paper can be accurate and still one-sided.

Warning Part A

Warning signs of a predatory journal

They publish anything for a fee. Reviewers do not exist. Your citation inherits their reputation.

01

The invitation came to you

Flattering emails asking you to submit or join the editorial board. Real journals rarely recruit undergraduates.

02

Publication in days

Peer review takes weeks or months. A promise of “rapid publication within 72 hours” means no review happened.

03

Vague scope, huge scope

A journal of “engineering, science, medicine and humanities” is not a journal. It is a website.

04

Not in Scopus or WoS

Search the journal name in Scopus. If it is not indexed, do not build your poster on it.

A journal website promises “rapid publication within 72 hours”. What does that tell you?

B

Reading abstracts and conclusions

How to decide in three minutes whether a paper is worth an hour.

Part B · Abstracts

An abstract in five moves

Almost every abstract in engineering follows this order. Find the move, and you find what you need.

  1. Background

    The topic and why it matters.

  2. Gap

    What is not yet known: the question the study asks.

  3. Method

    How the study was done: samples, period, conditions.

  4. Results

    What was found, usually with numbers and comparisons.

  5. Conclusion

    What the results mean and what should happen next.

Genre note: this is a “move” structure. The same five moves reappear in the introduction and conclusion of the full paper.

Part B · Abstracts

Read the abstract like an engineer

  1. Read the results move first

    Is there a number? Is there a comparison? If not, the paper may be an opinion piece.

  2. Then the method

    How many samples, over what period, in what conditions? 120 cells for 18 months is evidence. 3 cells for a week is a pilot.

  3. Then the gap

    Does their question match yours? A perfect study of the wrong question wastes your time.

  4. Only then download

    If all three pass, open the full paper and go straight to the conclusion.

A study tested 3 battery cells for one week. How should you treat it?

Part B · Conclusions

What a conclusion section gives you

01

The answer

The main claim in one or two sentences, usually stated more strongly than in the results. Quote it exactly before you paraphrase it.

02

The limits

Authors must say what their study could not show. “Results may not apply to cells above 60 Ah.” That limit is a gift: it tells you where your poster must be careful.

03

The next step

“Further work should…” tells you what the field does not yet know. Useful for a poster's own recommendation.

C

Paraphrasing

Rewriting someone else's idea in your own words, without losing the meaning and without stealing the words.

Part C · Why?

Why paraphrase at all

01

It proves you understood

Anyone can copy a sentence. Only someone who understood it can rewrite it correctly.

02

It fits your document

The source was written for its readers. Your poster has different readers, a different purpose and 40 words of space.

03

It keeps you honest

Copying without quotation marks is plagiarism even with a citation. Paraphrasing with a citation is scholarship.

You copy a sentence word for word, with no quotation marks, but you add a citation. Is that acceptable?

Part C · Three ways to use a source

Quote, paraphrase, summary

Three ways to use a source. All three need a citation. Only one uses the source's words.

Quote

The source's exact words, inside quotation marks.

Paraphrase

One idea in your own words, about the same length, with the meaning kept.

Summary

The main points only, in your own words, much shorter than the source.

Which of the three uses the source's own words?

Which of the three needs a citation?

Part C · Your toolkit

The four moves of paraphrasing

1

Change the word class

Nouns ↔ verbs.

2

Change the sentence structure

New starting point, active ↔ passive, split or join.

3

Synonyms, with care

Only for general words. Never alone.

4

Reorder the ideas

Change the order the facts arrive. Keep every fact.

  • I can name all four moves without looking.
  • I know which move is the most useful in technical English (Move 1).
  • I know that synonyms alone never make a paraphrase.
Part C · Move 1

Move 1: change the word class

Turning nouns into verbs (and verbs into nouns) is the single most useful paraphrasing move in technical English.

installing LED lighting → an LED retrofit
energy use → energy consumption

reduce (verb) → (noun)

publish (verb) → (noun)

measure (verb) → (noun)

observation (noun) → (verb)

installation (noun) → (verb)

Part C · Move 2

Move 2: change the sentence structure

Start from a different point. Swap active and passive. Split one long sentence or join two short ones.

Tropical temperatures roughly halve battery cycle life (Rahman et al., 2024).
Rahman et al. (2024) found that tropical temperatures roughly halve battery cycle life.

Same fact. The second version starts from the authors.

Other experts check peer-reviewed articles before publication. (active)
Peer-reviewed articles are checked by other experts before publication. (passive)
Peer review takes weeks or months. A promise of publication within 72 hours means no review happened.
Because peer review takes weeks or months, a promise of publication within 72 hours means no review happened.

“Peer-reviewed articles are checked by other experts.” → “Other experts check peer-reviewed articles.” Which change is this?

Part C · Move 3

Move 3: synonyms, with care

Part C · Move 4

Move 4: reorder the ideas

Change the order in which the facts arrive. Keep every fact.

Facts arrive: setting → effect

Peer review takes weeks or months, so a promise of rapid publication within 72 hours means no review happened.

Facts arrive: effect → reason

A promise of rapid publication within 72 hours means no review happened, because peer review takes weeks or months.

When you reorder the ideas, what must stay the same?

Part C · All four moves

A full worked example

All four moves in one sentence. Press the button to apply one move at a time.

Source: Peer-reviewed journal articles are checked by other experts before publication.

  1. Move 1 · Word class

    “before publication” (noun) → “before … are published” (verb).

  2. Move 2 · Structure

    Passive → active: “other experts” become the subject.

  3. Move 3 · Synonyms, with care

    The general verb “check” → “review”. The technical term “peer-reviewed” stays.

  4. Move 4 · Reorder

    The timing now arrives first.

    Before peer-reviewed journal articles are published, other experts review them.

Every fact kept: who checks (other experts), what (peer-reviewed journal articles), when (before publication).

Warning Part C

Patchwriting is still plagiarism

If you can lay your sentence over the source and the shape matches, you have not paraphrased.

Source

Peer review takes weeks or months.

Lay it over the source…

Peer checking needs weeks or months.

Same shape, two words swapped.

Is “Peer checking needs weeks or months” a paraphrase?

Part C · Citing

A paraphrase still needs a citation

The words are yours. The idea is not.

Tropical temperatures roughly halve battery cycle life (Rahman et al., 2024).
Rahman et al. (2024) found that tropical temperatures roughly halve battery cycle life.
Tropical temperatures roughly halve battery cycle life [3].

The reference list entry [3] then gives the full details.

Tropical temperatures roughly halve battery cycle life (Rahman et al., ).

(2024) found that tropical temperatures roughly halve battery cycle life.

Week 6 covers both styles in full. For now: every paraphrased fact on your poster carries a citation, no exceptions.
